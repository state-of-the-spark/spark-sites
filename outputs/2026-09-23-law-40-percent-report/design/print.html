1. Six in ten firms don’t pick up.

Here’s what that 2024 study found when it contacted 500 law firms across the United States:

Firms that answered the phone

40%

Firms that never answered or called back

48%

Firms that replied to an email inquiry

33%

Now think about who is calling. Someone was just arrested, or served, or told their marriage is over. They are scared, and they don’t call one lawyer. They call three, and they hire the first one who calls back and sounds like they know what to do next.

If your phone goes to voicemail at 12:15 while your assistant is at lunch, that person may already be sitting in another firm’s conference room by 2:00.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Call your own firm at 12:15 and again at 5:45, from a number your staff won’t recognize. Then fill out your website’s contact form at 9pm. Write down what happened each time. That’s what every new client experiences.

3. Know what one client is worth before you decide what marketing costs.

Most small firms have never done this math, so here it is with real Florida numbers. Typical flat fees Florida criminal defense firms publish:

Misdemeanor: $1,000 to $3,500

First-offense DUI: $2,500 to $7,500, often around $3,500

Third-degree felony drug charge: $3,500 to $10,000

And for family law, a contested Florida divorce commonly runs $5,000 to $20,000 or more. If about 7% of clicks become inquiries, and a small firm turns 8% to 15% of paid inquiries into clients, one new criminal defense client from Google Ads costs around $1,400 to $3,000. Against a $3,500 DUI fee, that still works. Barely.

Here’s the lever almost nobody pulls: the close rate. Answer faster and you close more of the same inquiries. That lowers your cost per client without spending another dollar on ads.

These are illustrative numbers built from published averages, not a prediction for your firm. Your own fees and close rate are the ones that matter.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Write down your average fee for your most common matter, and how many consultations it took you to sign your last ten clients. That’s your close rate. Every marketing decision should be measured against those two numbers.

4. You don’t need a bigger front desk. You need a faster first reply.

The fix for the 40% problem isn’t hiring someone to sit by the phone all day. It’s making sure no one who reaches out ever hears nothing. Here’s what that looks like done well:

Missed call? Within about a minute, the caller gets a short, professional text: sorry we missed you, we’ll call you back shortly, and what’s the best time to reach you.

Website form or email? An instant reply confirms you got it, asks two or three simple questions, and offers a consultation time.

Your team gets the alert on their phones, and a real person at your firm makes the callback.

Two rules keep this safe. The automatic replies never discuss the matter, and your firm approves every word before anything goes live. You still handle conflicts, confidentiality, and the actual conversation, like you always have.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Write the two sentences you’d want a scared stranger to read one minute after calling your office and getting no answer. Keep it warm, plain, and free of anything about their case. That’s your missed-call text.

5. Legal directories rent you your own clients.

Directories aren’t evil. A free, claimed profile on Avvo, Justia, or the Florida Bar’s own directory is worth ten minutes of your time.

The paid packages are a different story. Avvo’s paid tiers start around $49 to $100 a month, and the bigger all-in-one legal marketing contracts often lock small firms in for 12 to 24 months. One Connecticut firm sued Thomson Reuters after paying more than $28,000 on a $2,336-a-month FindLaw contract and never getting its promised new website launched.

The real problem is the same as renting leads in any business: the day you stop paying, it stops. You never owned any of it. You were renting a spot on someone else’s website, right next to your competitors.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Add up everything you paid directories and legal marketing platforms in the last 12 months. Divide by the clients you can actually trace back to them. If you can’t trace any, that’s your answer.

6. Three names on a map beat your whole ad budget.

That short local list is where the calls come from. You get into it the boring way, done all the way:

A Google Business Profile filled out completely, with every practice area listed as its own service.

Reviews coming in every month, with replies.

A website with one page per practice area, your towns in the page titles, and a name, address, and phone that match your Google profile exactly.

There’s a new front door too. People now ask ChatGPT and other AI assistants “who’s a good DUI lawyer in Lakeland?” Those tools lean on the same signals: clear practice-area pages, consistent listings, and reviews. That’s also the right order to market a law firm: profile, reviews, practice-area pages, a fast first reply, and only then paid ads.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Open your Google Business Profile. Add each practice area as its own service, set your service area to the counties you actually cover, and upload five real photos of your office and your team.

7. You can ask for reviews. Just do it the way the Bar allows.

Plenty of attorneys never ask for reviews because they’re not sure what’s allowed. So nothing happens, and the firm down the street with 140 reviews gets the call. Here’s how the Bar’s advertising rules touch this, in plain English (check the rules yourself; this is marketing, not legal advice):

Testimonials (Rule 4-7.13) have to be a real client’s own experience, not written or edited by you, and the client can’t receive anything of value for giving it.

Results. Don’t imply a guaranteed or predictable outcome. Past results need a clear disclaimer right next to them.

“Expert” and “specialist” (Rule 4-7.14) are off-limits unless you’re actually Board Certified in that area. “Focuses on” and “practices in” are safe.

Filing. Your website and blog are exempt from the Bar’s filing requirement, though the content still has to follow the rules. Many paid ads must be filed at least 20 days before they run.

For reviews, ask every satisfied client the same way, at the same moment (usually when the matter closes), with a direct link and nothing offered in return. When you reply, thank them without confirming they were a client or saying anything about the matter.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Pick the moment in your process when clients are happiest, usually the day their matter closes. Make asking for a Google review a standard step at that moment, with a text containing your direct review link.

8. You should own everything, and they should explain it.

Some marketing companies keep your website, your domain, your Google Business Profile, and your ad accounts under their own logins. Leave, and you start from zero. That’s not a partnership. That’s a hostage situation with a monthly invoice.

Everything should be in your firm’s name: the domain, the website, the Google Business Profile, the ad accounts, the reviews. Your marketing company should be added as a manager, not an owner.

And they should tell you what they did, why they did it, and what happened, in plain English, every month. A small firm needs four numbers:

01Calls
02Inquiries
03Consultations booked
04Clients signed

A report full of impressions and click-through rates is not results.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Check who is listed as the owner of your domain, your Google Business Profile, and your ad accounts. If it isn’t your firm, fix it this month.

09
