1. You’re not buying leads. You’re renting them.

Lead services sell the same homeowner request to several roofers at once. You pay whether you win the job or not, and you race the other roofers to the phone the second it lands.

Once you count the jobs you lose, a closed job from a shared-lead service costs $600 to $1,200.

The FTC ordered HomeAdvisor to pay up to $7.2 million after finding it misrepresented the quality, characteristics, and source of its leads.

But the price isn’t the real problem. The day you stop paying, the leads stop. You never owned any of it. You rented a phone number.

Owned leads come from three things you control: a Google Business Profile in the top three on the map, a steady stream of reviews, and a website that ranks for the towns you serve. They take a few months to build. Then your cost per lead drops toward zero, because nobody is charging you for the click.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Add up what you paid lead services in the last 12 months. Divide it by the jobs you actually closed from those leads. That’s your real cost per job. Measure every marketing decision against that number.

2. Their retainer was built for a bigger company than yours.

Traditional marketing agencies quote $3,000 to $5,000 a month. That’s $36,000 to $60,000 a year.

A full roof replacement in Florida runs about $18,000 to $32,000. So the retainer alone eats somewhere between 1.1 and 3.3 roofs of revenue a year. Before shingles. Before the crew. Before dump fees and the net-30 supplier bill. Shingle prices alone are up about 41% since 2020.

The tactics agencies sell do work. Ads work. SEO works. Content works. What doesn’t work is the budget. It was built for companies with a marketing department and six months of runway, not a one-to-four-crew roofing company making payroll while it waits on an adjuster’s check.

That’s the math nobody in my industry wants to talk about.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Ask any marketing company one question: “How many roof jobs do I need to sell each month to pay you back?” If they can’t answer in one sentence, they don’t understand your business.

3. The monthly report is not results.

You know the report. A PDF full of impressions, click-through rates, and graphs that go up and to the right. It looks busy. It tells you almost nothing. A roofer needs four numbers:

01Calls
02Form and text requests
03Inspections booked
04Jobs sold

Here’s an insider one. Calls from your website are not tracked unless someone sets it up on purpose. Google’s standard analytics does not count a tap on your phone number out of the box. So a lot of roofing sites have no idea how many calls they generate, and a lot of agency reports count what’s easy (clicks) instead of what’s true (calls).

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Ask whoever runs your marketing for last month’s calls, form requests, inspections booked, and jobs sold. If they can’t give you the first two, everything else in the report is a guess.

4. Three names on a map beat your whole ad budget.

That list sits above everything else, and it’s where the calls come from, especially the week after a storm. You get into it the boring way, done all the way:

A Google Business Profile filled out completely and kept active.

Reviews coming in every month, with replies.

A website whose name, address, and phone match the profile, with one page per service and your towns in the page titles.

That’s also the right order: profile, reviews, a website that converts, and only then ads. Most roofers start with ads and decide marketing doesn’t work.

Proof from a trade that runs on the same mechanics: when we did exactly this for Superior Spray, a Central Florida pest control company, search traffic climbed 276% in the first 90 days, and they hit their annual target in seven months.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Fill out your Google Business Profile completely: real hours, every service listed separately, your towns as the service area, and ten photos of real roofs your crew finished. Then text three happy customers for a review today.

6. The 15-year roof rule is the biggest marketing opening in Florida roofing.

Under SB 808, which took effect July 1, 2026, an insurer can’t refuse or non-renew a policy just because the roof is older than 15 years if a licensed inspector certifies at least five years of life left. And the law now lets roofing contractors perform that inspection themselves.

Every Florida homeowner with an aging roof and a letter from their carrier is looking for a roofer who understands the paperwork. Most marketing companies haven’t built a single page about it. What to build:

A page that answers “roof inspection for insurance,” with a button to book one.

Insurance inspections listed as their own service on your Google Business Profile.

Reviews that mention you helped with the insurance side.

This is marketing, not legal advice. Confirm the specifics with FRSA or your attorney before you promise a homeowner anything.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Add “insurance roof inspection” as a service on your Google Business Profile, and write one post explaining the 15-year rule in plain English, with your phone number at the end.

7. Storm chasers are beating you online, not on the roof.

Every big storm brings out-of-state crews who show up before local companies can mobilize, sign fast insurance jobs, and leave before the warranty calls start.

Being local only wins if the homeowner can see it. In the three seconds they spend on your website or Google profile, a local roofer and a storm chaser look exactly the same.

So show them. Your license number. “Locally owned since” and your year. Your towns by name. Real photos of your actual crew. Reviews from their neighbors.

In our scan, the best-performing post was a free back-to-school community event: 60 likes, 30 shares. Employee spotlights drew the most comments. Community and crew beat roof photos every time. Storm chasers can’t fake that. BAM.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Put your license number, how long you’ve been local, and a photo of your real crew at the top of your homepage and on your Google Business Profile.

8. You should own everything, and they should teach you.

Some marketing companies keep your website, your domain, your Google profile, and your ad accounts under their own logins. Leave, and you start from zero. That’s not a partnership. That’s a hostage situation with a monthly invoice.

Everything should be in your name. Your marketing company gets added as a manager, not an owner.

And here’s the bigger one. Our industry builds dependence and confusion on purpose. It’s how agencies keep clients on retainer for years without the client ever understanding what they’re paying for.

Break that. Hire people who explain what they did, why they did it, and what happened, in plain English, every month.

DO THIS THIS WEEK

Check who’s listed as the owner of your domain, your Google Business Profile, and your ad accounts. If it isn’t you, fix it this month.
