# Law Firm Ad Copy: 8 Meats + 3 CTAs

**Audience:** B2B. The reader owns or runs a solo or small Florida law firm (family
law, criminal defense, general practice), not a person shopping for an attorney.

**Source:** `spark-sites/outputs/2026-09-23-law-40-percent-report/the-40-percent-problem.md`
(the eight truths, every sourced number, the "do this this week" close of each section).

Voice matches the report itself: plain, direct, one person talking to one person,
"here's the truth," no hype, no em dashes, no guaranteed results, never calling
anyone an expert or specialist (Florida Bar Rule 4-7.14 says that word is off-limits
unless the attorney is Board Certified in that area, and the report itself makes
this point in Truth #7).

---

## Meats

### M1: Six in ten firms don't pick up (maps to Truth #1)

In 2024, a research firm posed as potential clients and called 500 law firms across
the country. Only 40% of them answered the phone. Five years earlier, it was 56%.
Another 48% never answered or called back at all, and only 33% replied to an email
inquiry, down from 40% in 2019.

Now think about who is calling a law firm. Someone was just arrested, or served, or
told their marriage is over. They are scared, and they don't call one firm. They
call three, and they hire the first one who calls back and sounds like they know
what to do next.

If a firm's phone goes to voicemail at 12:15 while the front desk is at lunch, that
caller may already be sitting in another firm's conference room by 2:00.

Here's the test: call the firm at 12:15 and again at 5:45, from a number staff won't
recognize, then fill out the website's contact form at 9pm. Write down what happens
each time. That's what every new client experiences, and it costs nothing to find
out.

**Rests on:** Clio 2024 Legal Trends Report secret-shopper study of 500 law firms
(40% answered, down from 56% in 2019; 48% never answered or returned calls; 33%
replied to email, down from 40% in 2019).

### M2: Every missed call is a paid click thrown away (maps to Truth #2)

Legal clicks are some of the most expensive in all of advertising. Right now, one
click on "criminal defense lawyer near me" runs about $16.73. "DUI lawyer near me"
runs $23.88. "Personal injury lawyer near me" runs $131.33.

A click isn't a call. On average, only about 7 of every 100 legal ad clicks turn
into an inquiry at all. So a $16.73 click works out to roughly $239 for every
person who actually reaches out. Then that person calls, and the phone rings out.

This is why so many firms say ads don't work. The ads worked. The front door
didn't.

Pull last month's call log from the phone provider. Count the calls that went to
voicemail or were never returned. Multiply that number by $239. That's roughly what
the missed calls cost in ad money that already left the account.

**Rests on:** Semrush US keyword data, September 2026 (CPC by search term); LocaliQ
Legal Search Advertising Benchmarks (~7% click-to-inquiry conversion).

### M3: Know what one client is worth before deciding what marketing costs (maps to Truth #3)

Most small firms have never done this math, so here it is with real Florida numbers.
A misdemeanor typically runs $1,000 to $3,500. A first-offense DUI runs $2,500 to
$7,500, often around $3,500. A third-degree felony drug charge runs $3,500 to
$10,000. A contested Florida divorce commonly runs $5,000 to $20,000 or more.

Now the other side. If about 7% of clicks become inquiries, and a small firm turns
somewhere between 8% and 15% of paid inquiries into clients, one new criminal
defense client from Google Ads costs somewhere around $1,400 to $3,000. Against a
$3,500 DUI fee, that still works. Barely.

Here's the lever almost nobody pulls: the close rate. Answer faster and a firm
closes more of the same inquiries it's already paying for, without spending another
dollar on ads.

Write down the average fee for the firm's most common matter, and how many
consultations it took to sign the last ten clients. That's the close rate. Every
marketing decision should be measured against those two numbers.

*These are illustrative numbers built from published averages, not a prediction for
any specific firm.*

**Rests on:** Florida criminal defense fee ranges (Litvack Law Group, Lotter Law,
Ansara Law); Florida divorce cost ranges (HelloDivorce, Cheshire Family Law); LEXGRO
Law Firm Lead Conversion Benchmarks (8% to 15% close rate for paid search).

### M4: The fix is a faster first reply, not a bigger front desk (maps to Truth #4)

The fix for the 40% problem isn't hiring someone to sit by the phone all day. It's
making sure no one who reaches out ever hears nothing.

Here's what that looks like done well. A missed call gets a short, professional text
back within about a minute: sorry we missed you, we'll call you back shortly, and
what's the best time to reach you. A website form or email gets an instant reply
that confirms it was received, asks two or three simple questions, and offers a
consultation time. The team gets the alert on their phones, and a real person at the
firm makes the callback.

Two rules keep this safe. The automatic replies never discuss the matter, and the
firm approves every word before anything goes live. The caller feels taken care of
within 60 seconds, and the firm has an hour to call back instead of three minutes.

Write the two sentences a scared stranger would want to read one minute after
calling and getting no answer. Keep it warm, plain, and free of anything about
their case. That's the missed-call text.

**Rests on:** the report's own missed-call-text-back and instant-form-reply design
(Truth #4), built to never discuss the matter and to require the firm's approval on
every word.

### M5: Legal directories rent a firm its own future clients (maps to Truth #5)

A free, claimed profile on Avvo, Justia, or the Florida Bar's own directory is worth
ten minutes of time. The paid packages are a different story. Avvo's paid tiers
start around $49 to $100 a month, and some firms report spending around $14,000 a
year once everything is added up. The bigger all-in-one legal marketing contracts
often lock small firms in for 12 to 24 months.

One Connecticut firm sued Thomson Reuters after paying more than $28,000 on a
$2,336-a-month FindLaw contract and never getting its promised new website
launched.

The real problem is the same as renting leads in any business: the day a firm stops
paying, it stops. Nobody owned any of it. It was a spot on someone else's website,
right next to the competition.

Add up everything paid to directories and legal marketing platforms in the last 12
months. Divide by the clients that can actually be traced back to them. If nothing
can be traced, that's the answer.

**Rests on:** Martindale-Avvo published directory pricing; the Bigger Law Firm and
Lawyerist reporting on the FindLaw/Thomson Reuters lawsuit and contract terms.

### M6: Three names on a map beat the whole ad budget (maps to Truth #6)

When someone nearby searches "divorce lawyer near me," Google doesn't show the whole
internet. It shows a short local list, usually three firms, above everything else.
That's the map pack, and it's where the calls come from.

Getting into it is the boring way, done all the way: a Google Business Profile
filled out completely, with every practice area listed as its own service; reviews
coming in every month, with replies; a website with one page per practice area
(divorce, custody, DUI, probate), the firm's towns in the page titles, and a name,
address, and phone number that match the Google profile exactly.

There's a new front door too. People now ask ChatGPT and other AI assistants "who's
a good DUI lawyer in Lakeland?" Those tools lean on the same signals: clear
practice-area pages, consistent listings, and reviews. The firms that are easy for
Google to read are the ones AI recommends.

That's the right order to market a law firm: profile, reviews, practice-area pages,
a fast first reply, and only then paid ads.

**Rests on:** the map pack structure described in the report (Truth #6); the same
NAP-consistency and practice-area-page mechanics that also drive AI-assistant
recommendations.

### M7: Ask for reviews the way the Bar allows (maps to Truth #7)

Plenty of attorneys never ask for reviews because they're not sure what's allowed.
So nothing happens, and the firm down the street with 140 reviews gets the call.

Here's how the Florida Bar's advertising rules touch this, in plain English. A
testimonial (Rule 4-7.13) has to be a real client's own experience, not written or
edited by the firm, and the client can't receive anything of value for giving it.
Results can't imply a guaranteed or predictable outcome, and past results need a
clear disclaimer right next to them. "Expert" and "specialist" (Rule 4-7.14) are off
the table unless the attorney is actually Board Certified in that area; "focuses on"
and "practices in" are safe. The website and blog are exempt from the Bar's filing
requirement, though the content still has to follow the rules, and many paid ads
must be filed with the Bar at least 20 days before they run.

For reviews, that means asking every satisfied client the same way, at the same
moment, usually when the matter closes, with a direct link and nothing offered in
return. When replying, thank them without confirming they were a client or saying
anything about the matter.

Pick the moment clients are happiest, usually the day the matter closes. Make
asking for a Google review a standard step at that moment, with a text containing a
direct review link.

*This is marketing, not legal advice. Check the current Bar rules before relying on
any of it.*

**Rests on:** The Florida Bar's Advertising Filing Requirements page and the 2025
Handbook on Lawyer Advertising (Rules 4-7.13, 4-7.14, 4-7.19, 4-7.20).

### M8: Own everything, and they should explain it (maps to Truth #8)

Some marketing companies keep a firm's website, domain, Google Business Profile,
and ad accounts under their own logins. Leave, and the firm starts from zero.
That's not a partnership. That's a hostage situation with a monthly invoice.

Everything should be in the firm's own name: the domain, the website, the Google
Business Profile, the ad accounts, the reviews. The marketing company should be
added as a manager, not an owner.

And they should explain what they did, why they did it, and what happened, in plain
English, every month. A small firm needs four numbers: calls, inquiries,
consultations booked, and clients signed. A report full of impressions and
click-through rates is not results.

Check who is listed as the owner of the domain, the Google Business Profile, and
the ad accounts. If it isn't the firm, that's worth fixing this month.

**Rests on:** the report's ownership-and-transparency standard (Truth #8): domain,
website, GBP and ad accounts in the client's name; the four numbers (calls,
inquiries, consultations, clients signed) as the only report that matters.
