# The 40% Problem: 8 Recorded Video Scripts + 3 Cutdowns

**Format (Deep Ad shape, recorded-video version):** Grant on camera, one truth per
video, 45 to 75 seconds. Each script gives a hook line (the first 3 seconds, said
AND shown as on-screen text so it lands with sound off), the full spoken script,
B-roll/shot notes, the on-screen caption track, a caption for the post itself, and
which ad hook it pairs with (so the video and the static ad testing the same idea
reinforce each other in the feed).

**Voice:** plain, direct, one person talking to one person, Grant's educator voice
from the report's own "Read this first" section. No em dashes. No guaranteed
results. Never "expert" or "specialist" (Florida Bar Rule 4-7.14 says that's off
limits unless Board Certified, and this report is about the report's own subject
matter, so it holds itself to the same rule).

## Script 1: Six in ten firms don't pick up (Truth #1)

**Working title:** The Phone Test

**Hook line (0:00-0:03, on-screen):** "Only 40% of law firms answer the phone."

**Full script:**
In 2024, a research firm called 500 law firms across the country pretending to be
new clients. Only 40% of them answered the phone. Five years earlier, it was 56%.

Think about who's calling a law firm. Somebody was just arrested, or served, or
told their marriage is over. They're scared, and they don't call one firm. They
call three, and they hire whoever calls back first.

Here's a test that costs nothing. Call your own firm at 12:15, from a number staff
won't recognize. Call again at 5:45. Then fill out your own website's contact form
at 9pm. Write down what happens each time. That's what every new client
experiences right now.

I wrote a free report with seven more truths like this one, sourced, no pitch
required until the very end. Link's below.

**B-roll / shot notes:** Grant seated at a desk, phone visible on the desk in
frame. Cut to a close-up of a phone ringing unanswered (silent, ringing screen)
around the "56%" line. Cut to a hand filling out a contact form on a laptop at the
"9pm" line, screen dim/nighttime lighting to sell "after hours."

**On-screen captions (key lines, timed to speech):**
- "40% answered the phone (2024 study, 500 firms)"
- "Down from 56% in 2019"
- "Call at 12:15. Call at 5:45. Try the form at 9pm."

**Caption for the post:** A 2024 study called 500 law firms pretending to be new
clients. Only 40% answered the phone, down from 56% five years earlier. Try it on
your own firm this week; the free report has seven more truths like it.
https://sparkmysite.com/law-firm-report/

**Pairs with ad hook:** H26: "How many consultations does a missed call cost a
small law firm every month?"

## Script 2: Every missed call is a paid click thrown away (Truth #2)

near me" runs $23.88 a click. "Personal injury lawyer near me" runs $131.33.

A click isn't a call. Only about 7 out of every 100 legal ad clicks turn into an
inquiry at all. So that $23.88 click actually costs closer to $239 for the one

Then that person calls, and the phone rings out.

This is why firms say ads don't work. The ad worked. The front door didn't.

Pull last month's call log. Count the calls that went to voicemail or never got a
callback. Multiply by $239. That's roughly what already left your ad budget for
nothing.

The full math, with sources, is in the free report. Link's below.

**On-screen captions:**

## Script 3: Know what one client is worth before deciding what marketing costs (Truth #3)

**Working title:** Do The Math First

**Hook line (0:00-0:03, on-screen):** "What's one client actually worth to you?"

**Full script:**
Most small firms have never done this math. Here it is with real Florida numbers.

A first-offense DUI runs $2,500 to $7,500, often around $3,500. A contested Florida
divorce commonly runs $5,000 to $20,000 or more.

Now the other side. If about 7% of clicks become inquiries, and a small firm closes
somewhere between 8 and 15% of paid inquiries, one new criminal defense client from
Google Ads costs somewhere around $1,400 to $3,000. Against a $3,500 DUI fee, that
still works. Barely.

Here's the lever almost nobody pulls: the close rate. Answer faster, close more of
the leads you're already paying for, without spending another ad dollar.

Write down your average fee and how many consultations it took to sign your last
ten clients. That's your real number, and every marketing decision should get
measured against it.

**B-roll / shot notes:** Simple whiteboard or notepad math shown in close-up (Grant
writing the numbers by hand: fee range, then close rate, then cost per client).
Keep this one desk-bound and low-key; it's the "math" video, so let the numbers
carry it rather than motion.

**On-screen captions:**
- "DUI fee: $2,500-$7,500"
- "Divorce: $5,000-$20,000+"
- "Cost per client: ~$1,400-$3,000"
- "*Illustrative averages, not a promise for your firm"

**Caption for the post:** Before judging a marketing bill, know what one client is
worth. A DUI runs $2,500 to $7,500 in Florida; a contested divorce runs $5,000 to
$20,000+. Do that math before the ad math. Free report:
https://sparkmysite.com/law-firm-report/

**Pairs with ad hook:** H10: "Family law firm in Central Florida, a contested

## Script 4: The fix is a faster first reply, not a bigger front desk (Truth #4)

**Working title:** The One-Minute Text

**Hook line (0:00-0:03, on-screen):** "A missed call doesn't have to mean a lost client."

**Full script:**
The fix for the missed-call problem isn't hiring someone to sit by the phone all
day. It's making sure nobody who reaches out ever hears nothing back.

Here's what that looks like. A missed call gets a short text within about a
minute: sorry we missed you, we'll call you back shortly, what's the best time to
reach you. A web form gets an instant reply that books a consultation time. Your
team gets the alert, and a real person at your firm makes the actual callback.

Two rules keep it safe. The automatic reply never discusses the case, and your
firm approves every word before it goes live.

The caller feels taken care of in 60 seconds. Your firm gets an hour to call back
instead of three minutes.

**B-roll / shot notes:** Phone screen close-up showing a missed call turning into
an outgoing text (mock UI is fine, keep it generic, no real client name). Cut back
to Grant for the "two rules keep it safe" line, said slightly slower and direct to
camera since it's the compliance reassurance line.

**On-screen captions:**
- "Missed call -> text back in ~1 minute"
- "Never discusses the matter"
- "Firm approves every word"
- "A real person still makes the callback"

**Caption for the post:** The fix for a missed call isn't a bigger front desk. It's
a text back in about a minute that never discusses the case, approved word for
word by the firm, so a real person still makes the callback. Free report:
https://sparkmysite.com/law-firm-report/

**Pairs with ad hook:** H03: "A missed call at 12:15 can cost a Florida firm a
signed client by 2:00 the same afternoon."

worth ten minutes of your time. The paid tiers are a different story. Avvo's paid
plans start around $49 to $100 a month, and some firms report spending around
$14,000 a year once it's all added up.

One Connecticut firm sued Thomson Reuters after paying more than $28,000 on a
$2,336-a-month FindLaw contract, and never got the website it was promised.

The problem is the same as renting leads anywhere. The day you stop paying, it
stops. You never owned any of it. You were renting a spot on someone else's
website, next to your competitors.

Add up what you paid directories last year. Divide by the clients you can actually
trace back to them. If you can't trace any, that's your answer.

## Script 7: Ask for reviews the way the Bar allows (Truth #7)

**Working title:** Reviews, Done By The Rules

**Hook line (0:00-0:03, on-screen):** "The Florida Bar lets you ask for reviews. Here's how."

**Full script:**
Plenty of attorneys never ask for reviews because they're not sure what's allowed.
So nothing happens, and the firm down the street with 140 reviews gets the call.

Here's the plain-English version. A testimonial has to be the client's own words,
not written or edited by you, and they can't get anything of value for giving it.
Results can't imply a guaranteed outcome, and past results need a clear disclaimer
next to them. And words like "expert" or "specialist" are off the table unless
you're actually Board Certified in that area.

For reviews, that means asking every satisfied client the same way, at the same
moment, usually when the matter closes, with a direct link, nothing offered in
return.

This is marketing, not legal advice, so check the current Bar rules yourself. But
the lane is open, and almost nobody's using it.

**B-roll / shot notes:** Grant seated, calm delivery (this is the compliance-heavy
script; keep visuals simple, maybe a phone showing a text with a review link being
sent, generic mockup only). No dramatic B-roll needed; let the plain-English
explanation carry it.

**On-screen captions:**
- "Testimonials: the client's own words, nothing offered in return"
- "No guaranteed-outcome language"
- "'Expert' / 'specialist' = Board Certified only (Rule 4-7.14)"
- "This is marketing, not legal advice"

**Caption for the post:** The Florida Bar does let attorneys ask for reviews, the
same way, at the same moment, with nothing offered in return. Most small firms
just aren't doing it. Free report (with the specific rules cited):
https://sparkmysite.com/law-firm-report/

**Pairs with ad hook:** H13: "The Florida Bar allows something about asking for
reviews that almost no small firm actually uses."

## Script 8: Own everything, and they should explain it (Truth #8)

**Working title:** Whose Login Is It?

**Hook line (0:00-0:03, on-screen):** "Who owns your Google profile, you or your agency?"

**Full script:**
Some marketing companies keep your website, your domain, your Google Business
Profile, and your ad accounts under their own logins. Leave, and you start from
zero.

That's not a partnership. That's a hostage situation with a monthly invoice.

Here's how it's supposed to work. The domain is in your name. The website is in
your name. The Google Business Profile is in your name. The ad accounts are in
your name. Your marketing company gets added as a manager, not the owner.

And they should tell you what they did, why, and what happened, in plain English,
every month. A small firm needs four numbers: calls, inquiries, consultations
booked, clients signed. A report full of impressions and click-through rates isn't
results.

Go check right now who's listed as the owner of your domain, your profile, your ad
accounts. If it isn't you, that's worth fixing this month.

**B-roll / shot notes:** Screen recording of a generic domain/GBP ownership settings
page (mock or blurred, no real client account shown). Direct-to-camera close-up for
the "four numbers" line, since it's the most quotable line in the script.

**On-screen captions:**
- "Domain, website, GBP, ad accounts: in YOUR firm's name"
- "Agency = manager, not owner"
- "Four numbers that matter: calls, inquiries, consultations, clients signed"

**Caption for the post:** Check who's actually listed as the owner of your domain,
your Google Business Profile, and your ad accounts. If it isn't your firm, that's
worth fixing this month, before signing anything else. Free report:
https://sparkmysite.com/law-firm-report/

**Pairs with ad hook:** H28: "Who actually owns a firm's Google Business Profile,
its website, and its ad account?" (Cutdown/alternate version can lead with H31,
"Some marketing companies keep a law firm's domain and Google Business Profile
under their own login," as a statement-form open instead of a question.)
