## Meats

### M1: You're renting your leads (maps to Truth #1)

You've got a good crew. Five star reviews. And every month you're still paying for the same homeowner three other roofers already got. That's how the lead services work. A shared lead from a service like Angi can get sold to as many as sixteen contractors at once. You're not buying a lead, you're buying a spot in a race, and you're paying whether you win the job or not.

Run the real math. A paid search lead in home services runs about $228 on average. Once you count every job you bid and lose, a closed job from a shared lead service actually costs somewhere between $600 and $1,200. And that's before you get to what happened to HomeAdvisor: the FTC ordered them to pay up to $7.2 million after finding they misrepresented the quality and the source of their leads.

Here's the part that stings the most. The day you stop paying, the leads stop. You never owned any of it. You rented a phone number for a while.

Owned leads work different. They come from your Google Business Profile ranking in the top three, reviews coming in steady, and a website that ranks for your towns. It takes months to build. But once it's built, your cost per lead drops toward zero, because nobody's charging you for the click anymore.

**Rests on:** shared Angi leads sold to up to 16 contractors; $228 average paid-search lead; $600 to $1,200 real cost per closed job; FTC's up-to-$7.2M order against HomeAdvisor.

### M2: What the retainer really costs you (maps to Truth #2)

A typical marketing agency wants $3,000 to $5,000 a month from you. Add that up and it's $36,000 to $60,000 a year, gone before you've bought a single shingle.

Now put that next to your business. A full roof replacement in Florida runs somewhere between $18,000 and $32,000. So just to break even on that retainer, before payroll, before dump fees, before your net-30 bill comes due, you need to sell somewhere between 1.1 and 3.3 full replacements a year just to pay the marketing company. Not to make money. Just to cover the invoice.

And material costs aren't sitting still either. Shingle prices are up about 41% since 2020, which means the roofs you need to sell to cover that retainer keep getting more expensive to actually build.

None of this means the tactics don't work. Ads work. SEO works. Content works. The problem isn't the tactic, it's the budget. That retainer was priced for a company with a marketing department and six months of runway sitting in the bank. You're a one to four crew operation making payroll while you wait on an adjuster's check to clear. Nobody built that retainer with your cash flow in mind, because the agency that built it was never thinking about your cash flow at all.

**Rests on:** agency retainers of $3,000 to $5,000/mo ($36k to $60k/yr); Florida roof replacement cost $18,000 to $32,000; shingle prices up about 41% since 2020.

### M3: The report that tells you nothing (maps to Truth #3)

You've seen the report. A PDF full of impressions and click-through rates, graphs climbing up and to the right. It looks like something is happening. Read it twice and you still can't answer the one question you actually care about: did we sell any roofs.

A roofer only needs four numbers out of any marketing report. Calls. Form and text requests. Inspections booked. Jobs sold. That's it. Everything else is decoration.

Here's the part most roofers don't know. Calls from your website are not tracked unless somebody sets that up on purpose. Google's standard analytics does not count a tap on your phone number out of the box. So there's a decent chance your own website has no idea how many calls it's actually generating for you right now, today. And if your marketing report doesn't show calls, it's counting what's easy to measure, not what's true.

Next time you get a report, or next time you talk to whoever runs your marketing, ask for last month's calls, form requests, inspections booked, and jobs sold. If they can hand you the first two without stalling, that tells you something. If they can't, everything else in that pretty PDF is a guess dressed up as data.

**Rests on:** the four numbers a roofer actually needs (calls, form/text requests, inspections booked, jobs sold); calls not tracked by standard analytics unless set up on purpose.

### M4: Three names beat your ad budget (maps to Truth #4)

When somebody near you searches roofer near me, Google doesn't show them the whole internet. It shows a short list, usually three businesses, sitting above everything else. That's the map pack. That's where the calls come from, especially the week after a storm rolls through.

Getting into that list isn't a trick. It's a Google Business Profile filled out completely and kept active. Reviews coming in every month, with replies. A website whose name, address, and phone match your profile exactly, with a separate page for replacement, repair, storm damage, and metal, with your towns named in the page titles.

That's also the order marketing is supposed to happen in. Profile first, reviews second, a website that actually converts third, and only then paid ads. Most roofers do it backwards. They start with ads, spend a couple thousand dollars proving their website doesn't turn visitors into inspection requests, and walk away deciding marketing just doesn't work for them.

We've seen the mechanics play out in a different trade that runs on the same local, truck, phone setup. When we did exactly this, in that order, for a Central Florida pest control company, their search traffic climbed 276% in the first ninety days. They hit their annual target in seven months.

Three names in a box beat whatever you're spending on ads right now, if those three names aren't yours.

**Rests on:** the map pack showing roughly 3 local businesses above everything else; the profile, reviews, website, then ads sequence; Superior Spray's 276% search traffic increase in 90 days and hitting its annual target in 7 months.

### M5: Whoever answers first wins the storm lead (maps to Truth #5)

After a storm comes through, a homeowner doesn't call one roofer and wait. They fill out three forms in ten minutes and see who calls back first. Whoever answers first usually gets the inspection.

Here's how people actually behave with their phones. 84% of people hang up on calls from numbers they don't recognize. 72% would rather text a business than call or email it. When somebody does text, the average reply they expect comes back in about 90 seconds. When they email, they're used to waiting about 90 minutes for anything back.

Now think about your own setup. If a form on your website drops into an inbox you check at 9pm, you lost that lead at 6:15, hours before you even opened your laptop. It doesn't matter how good your crew is or how fair your price is if somebody else already booked the inspection while you were still finishing the job in front of you.

The fix isn't complicated. Every form and every missed call gets a text back within about a minute, day or night, with an easy way to book the inspection right there. Then when you do call them, it's from a number they've already seen once already.

Try it yourself tonight. Fill out your own website form like a homeowner would and time how long it takes for anyone to respond. That's the exact experience every lead you're paying for goes through right now.

**Rests on:** 84% hang up on unrecognized numbers; 72% prefer texting a business; text reply ~90 seconds vs. email reply ~90 minutes.

### M6: The 15-year roof rule nobody's marketing yet (maps to Truth #6)

Florida passed a law that changes the whole conversation around an aging roof. SB 808 took effect July 1, 2026. Under it, an insurance company can't refuse to renew a policy just because the roof is over 15 years old, as long as a licensed inspector certifies it still has at least five years of life left. And here's the part that matters to you specifically: the law now lets roofing contractors do that inspection themselves.

Think about what that creates. Every homeowner in Florida with an aging roof and a letter from their insurance carrier is out there right now searching for one thing, a roofer who understands the paperwork side of this. A few roofers have already started selling this as an insurance compliance inspection. Almost no marketing company has built a single page explaining it in plain English.

That's an open lane. Right now, today, while most of your competitors' websites don't mention SB 808 at all.

Add insurance roof inspection as its own listed service on your Google Business Profile. Write one post explaining the 15-year rule the way you'd explain it to a neighbor over the fence, with your phone number at the bottom. This is marketing, not legal advice, so confirm the exact language with FRSA or your attorney before you promise a homeowner anything specific. But the opening itself is real, and it's sitting wide open right now.

**Rests on:** SB 808, effective July 1, 2026, barring insurers from refusing/non-renewing policies over a roof's age past 15 years when a licensed inspector certifies 5+ years of life left, and allowing roofing contractors to perform that inspection.

### M7: Storm chasers win online, not on the roof (maps to Truth #7)

Every big storm brings the same thing behind it. Out-of-state crews show up before local companies can even mobilize, sign fast insurance jobs, and leave town before the first warranty call comes in.

You'd think being local automatically wins that fight. It doesn't, unless the homeowner can actually see it. In the three seconds somebody spends looking at your website or your Google profile, a local roofer and a storm chaser look exactly the same. Same stock photos. Same generic promises. Same nothing that says who you actually are.

So show them who you are. Your license number. How long you've been local and in what town. Your actual service area named by city. Real photos of your real crew on a real roof. Reviews from their actual neighbors.

We scanned 210 Facebook posts from 10 roofing companies here in Polk County to see what actually gets attention. The best performing post in the whole sample wasn't a finished roof. It was a free back-to-school community event, and it pulled 60 likes and 30 shares. Employee spotlights, the kind that name a real person on your crew, drew the most comments of anything we found. Community and crew beat roof photos every single time.

A storm chaser can't fake being from here. They can't fake a crew somebody's neighbor already knows. Show it, and you already win the fight.

**Rests on:** storm chasers arriving before local companies mobilize; the 210-post Facebook scan of 10 Polk County roofers; the back-to-school community post's 60 likes and 30 shares; employee spotlights drawing the most comments.

### M8: Your name should be on everything (maps to Truth #8)

Some marketing companies keep your website, your domain, your Google Business Profile, and your ad accounts under their own logins, not yours. Leave them, and you start over from zero. Every review, every ranking, every dollar you spent building it stays behind with them. That's not a partnership. That's a hostage situation with a monthly invoice attached to it.

Here's how it's supposed to work. The domain is in your name. The website is in your name. The Google Business Profile is in your name. The ad accounts are in your name. Your marketing company gets added as a manager on those accounts, not as the owner of them.

There's a bigger pattern behind this, and it's worth saying plainly. This industry builds dependence and confusion on purpose. Confusion is how an agency keeps a client on retainer for years without that client ever fully understanding what they're actually paying for or how any of it works.

You can check this yourself in about ten minutes. Go look at who's listed as the owner of your domain, your Google Business Profile, and your ad accounts right now. If it isn't you, that's not a small technicality to fix eventually. That's the thing to fix this month, before you sign anything else with anybody.

**Rests on:** agencies holding a client's domain/website/GBP/ad accounts under their own logins; the report's claim that the industry builds dependence and confusion on purpose.
